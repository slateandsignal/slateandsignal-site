06
Signal
Blind Spot
You're solving a problem people don't realize they have until it's already painful.

Your answers show a clear frustration that people don't make time for bodywork despite the damage they do to themselves every day. You see massage as preventive maintenance. Most people see it as a treat they book when their back finally gives out.

The gap is that your ideal client doesn't know they need you yet. They're not searching for a massage therapist while their shoulders are still functional. They're ignoring the tightness until it becomes pain. That means your content has to do two jobs: convince people they have a problem worth solving before it gets worse, and position you as the person who can solve it.

Right now, your answers focus on what you do and why you love it. That's important. What's missing is the part where you articulate the cost of not doing anything. What happens to someone's body after six months of sitting at a desk with no bodywork? What does that tightness turn into? How much worse does it get? That's the part that makes someone book an appointment before they're in crisis.

Your brand will be stronger when you can name the specific problems you prevent. The more concrete you can make the cost of waiting, the easier it is for people to see why they should book now instead of later.

07
First
Move
Start posting what you already know.

You were top of your class in massage school. You love learning about bodywork. You have answers to questions people don't even know to ask yet. Your first move is to start sharing that knowledge in short, simple posts or videos. Pick one thing you explain to clients all the time and record yourself saying it out loud. Post it. Do that once or twice a week.

You don't need a content calendar or a filming setup. You need to get comfortable talking about what you know in a way that helps people understand why it matters. The format is secondary. The point is to build the habit of showing up and teaching something useful.

At the same time, track what questions your actual clients ask you during sessions. Those questions are content. If one person is asking, ten more people are wondering the same thing and not saying it out loud. Write them down. Answer them in your next post.

Your goal for the next three months is not to build a massive following. Your goal is to establish a baseline rhythm of posting useful information and to start seeing which topics get the most response. That feedback will tell you what to double down on. You'll refine the angles as you go. For now, just start talking about what you already know.

Visual
Identity
A compass for the visual work ahead. Direction for how the visual world should feel.
Mood
Grounded and Approachable
Your visual mood should feel calm, warm, and unpolished in the right way. Think natural light, simple backgrounds, earth tones showing up in what you wear and where you film. Nothing overly styled or spa-like. You're not a luxury retreat, you're a skilled practitioner who comes to people's homes and helps them feel better. The visuals should reinforce that: capable, grounded, human.
Photography Style
Natural Light, Minimal Staging
Photos and videos should look like they were taken in real spaces. Natural light, simple backgrounds, you talking to the camera or showing your workspace. No need for professional photography. The goal is to look like someone who knows what they're doing and is comfortable in their own work.
Color Direction
Earthy and Warm
Sage green, warm beige, and terracotta create a grounded, restorative palette that feels like a well-worn yoga studio or a quiet corner of someone's home: capable, warm, and human.
Tone of Voice
Direct and Knowledgeable
You talk like someone who knows what they're doing and isn't trying to sell you on something you don't need. Your tone should be straightforward, warm, and confident. You explain things clearly. You don't overcomplicate. You sound like a practitioner. When you recommend something, it's because it works.
