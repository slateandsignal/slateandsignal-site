Signal
Blind Spot
You're trying to build something private on a public platform.

You're energized by family and church work, but those are internal projects. They're not designed to be content, and trying to force them into that shape might hollow them out.

At the same time, the things people already come to you for (administration advice, automotive troubleshooting) don't seem to be what you want to build a brand around. They're skills, but they're not the thing you're passionate about. The result is a tension: the stuff you care about most isn't public-facing, and the stuff people recognize you for isn't what you want to lead with.

This isn't a fatal problem, but it does mean you need to decide what you're willing to make public and what stays private. If your faith, your family, and your church work are the core of who you are, then your content needs to be an extension of that. That might mean writing about LDS theology and history, sharing the principles you're teaching your kids, or documenting the discipline and work ethic you learned from the Marines and farm labor. It means making the private thing public in a way that feels right to you.

If you're not willing to do that, then your content needs to be built around one of the other directions: psychology and human behavior, practical mentorship, or even the technical troubleshooting skills people already come to you for. But right now, you're stuck in the middle. That's the blind spot. You need to pick a lane, and then build everything else around that decision.

07
First
Move
Write ten reflections and see which one feels like you.

Your first move is to write ten short pieces, each one exploring a different angle from your answers. One about a principle from LDS history or scripture. One about a human behavior observation. One about a lesson from farm work or the Marines. One about a repair or project you completed. One about something you learned from fatherhood. One quoting a philosopher and connecting it to something you're thinking about now. Keep them short, 300 to 500 words, just enough to develop the idea.

The goal is to see which one feels like the thing you could write a hundred more of without getting bored. Which one sounds like you. Which one makes you want to keep going instead of feeling like an assignment. That's the direction.

Once you know which direction feels right, write ten more in that vein. Build a small body of work before you worry about an audience. Let the repetition clarify the voice, the angle, and the through-line. Then, and only then, start thinking about where to post it and who it's for.

This approach works because you're someone who thinks by writing and pondering. You're going to figure it out by doing the work and seeing what emerges. So do the work first. The rest will clarify itself.

Visual
Identity
A compass for the visual work ahead. Direction for how the visual world should feel.
Mood
Grounded, serious, built to last.
Your visual mood should feel like work clothes and well-worn tools. Practical, durable, unpretentious. Think weathered wood, concrete, metal hardware, hands holding something real. The aesthetic is earned. It's the look of someone who's done the labor and has the scars to prove it. Textures matter more than decoration. Everything should feel like it has weight and history.
Photography Style
Documentary.
Your photography should be straightforward and unadorned. Natural light, real settings, no artificial styling. If you're showing a project, show the workspace as it actually is: tools on the bench, sawdust on the floor, the thing half-finished. If you're showing a quote or a principle, pair it with an image that grounds it in the physical world: a book on a table, a landscape, a building, something tangible. The style is observational. You're documenting.
Color Direction
Earth, iron, and deep blue.
Grounded and serious, built from deep navy authority, warm terracotta earth, and amber accents on a cream foundation: the palette of earned credibility.
Tone of Voice
Direct, observational, earned.
Your tone should be the voice of someone who's thought about this deeply and lived it personally. You don't need to prove anything or perform authority. You say what you've noticed, what you've learned, and what you think, and you let the reader decide if it's useful. You're comfortable with silence and with letting an idea sit. You use quotes because they clarify your thinking. You're not trying to be likable, you're trying to be clear. The voice is steady, grounded, and unafraid of being wrong, because you're more interested in the truth than in being right.
