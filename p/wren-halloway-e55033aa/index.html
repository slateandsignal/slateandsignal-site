06
Signal
Blind Spot
You assume people know you are not selling extreme sport

Your frustration with outdoor sport being sold as elite and extreme is clear in your answers. That is the entire point of your work. The gap is that you have not yet built a visible body of content that makes that framing obvious to someone who does not already know you.

Right now, if someone stumbles across your work, they have no way to tell whether you are selling cold water swimming as a hardcore challenge or as something gentle and possible. The difference between those two framings is everything. One attracts people who want to prove something. The other attracts people who are nervous and need permission to start small. You are trying to reach the second group, but without content that explicitly names that, you risk attracting the first.

This matters because your coaching offer depends on trust. A nervous adult will not book a session with you unless they believe you understand their fear and will not rush them past it. That belief has to be built in public, in small pieces, over time. You cannot assume people will infer it from the fact that you exist. You have to say it plainly, repeatedly, in different shapes, until it is the first thing someone thinks when they hear your name.

The fix is straightforward. Start posting content that explicitly positions cold water swimming as something ordinary people can do. Name the fear. Name the bad advice. Show what it looks like to start small and stay. Do that enough times and the framing becomes inseparable from your name. That is when your small group sessions fill without advertising, because the right people already know what you stand for before they ever reach out.

07
First
Move
Post one piece of first-minute coaching every week

You said you want to talk about the first minute in cold water and how to get through it. That is your starting point. Your first move is to post one short piece of coaching every week, talking directly to camera about one specific thing people ask you: what to do with the gasp reflex, whether a wetsuit helps, how to breathe through the panic, what to do if you want to turn around.

Each one should be 60 seconds or less. Talk to camera for 45 seconds, then cut to 10 or 15 seconds of unnarrated swim footage. No music, no voiceover, just the sound of the water. End with a single line of text that names the point. Post it as a Reel or a Short. Do this every week for three months and you will have twelve pieces of coaching content that make it obvious what you do and who it is for.

The weekly letter you mentioned can come later. Right now, you need a visible body of work that builds trust with nervous adults who do not yet know you. The letter is a retention tool for people who are already following you. The short coaching videos are the acquisition tool that gets them there in the first place. Start with the videos. Once you have a consistent posting rhythm and a small audience, add the letter.

Do not wait to have a full content plan or a perfect posting schedule. Start with one video this week. Talk about the first thing people ask you. Film it on your phone. Post it. Do it again next week. The consistency matters more than the production quality, and the specificity matters more than the reach. You are trying to build a body of work that makes the right people feel seen and safe enough to reach out.

08
Visual
Identity
A compass for the visual work ahead. Direction for how the visual world should feel.
Mood
Quiet, competent, and unglamorous
Your visual world should feel like early morning water, worn equipment, and the kind of coastal landscape that is not trying to sell you anything. Think weathered rope, painted hull red, chipped enamel, the light at dawn before anyone else is awake. Nothing polished or aspirational. Nothing that looks like a travel ad or an athletic brand. The mood is quiet competence, the aesthetic of someone who knows how to tie a knot and check the tide table and get in the water when it is cold. Unglamorous in the best sense. Real, worn-in, and trustworthy.
Photography Style
Low, still, and unfiltered
Shoot low to the water. Shoot in flat morning light or dusk. Avoid dramatic angles, bright colors, or anything that makes cold water swimming look heroic or extreme. Show the swim itself without narration or music. Show the wetsuit hanging on a hook, the boat pulled up on the beach, the harbour wall at dawn. Keep everything still and plain. No motion blur, no action shots, no one leaping off a rock. The photography should feel like documentation. Someone watching should think 'I could do that,' not 'I could never do that.'
Color Direction
Harbour blue, weathered rope, hull red, enamel dusk
Harbour blue, weathered rope, hull red, and enamel dusk: a coastal working palette built for quiet competence.
Tone of Voice
Plain, steady, and unrushed
You talk like someone who has done the thing and is now walking someone else through it. No hype, no urgency, no motivational language. You do not tell people to push through or get over it. You tell them what happens in the first minute, what to do with their breathing, and what it looks like when they stay. Your tone is steady, practical, and unrushed. You sound like someone who knows how to wait, who understands that fear is not a weakness, and who will not make someone feel small for being nervous. Write the way you would talk to someone standing at the water's edge who is not sure they can do it. Short sentences. Plain words. No drama. Just the next step, and then the one after that.
