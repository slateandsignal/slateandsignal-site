06
Signal
Blind Spot
You're holding back the specifics that would make people trust you

Your answers show that you know what you're doing in the treatment room. You've helped people recover from frozen shoulder, you work with post-surgery clients, and you have real training in bodywork. But when it comes to sharing that publicly, you're staying general. You talk about recovery tools and helping people heal, but you don't name the specific techniques you use, the specific conditions you treat, or the specific outcomes people can expect when they work with you.

The risk here is that your content ends up sounding like every other wellness account. People need to know what makes you different. They need to know what you actually do, what you've seen work, and why your approach is worth paying attention to. The frozen shoulder case is a perfect example of the kind of specificity that builds trust. That's the kind of detail that makes someone think, I have that exact problem, and this person has solved it before.

You don't need to give away your entire process for free. You just need to show people that you have one. Name the condition. Name the technique. Name the outcome. That's what turns a general statement about healing into a reason someone books a session with you instead of scrolling past.

Your preference for writing on your own timeline without explanation is fine, but it can't become an excuse to stay vague. The best written content is still specific. It still names the thing. That's the level of detail that works.

07
First
Move
Write three recovery case posts and share them

Your first move is to write three short posts, each one a real recovery case from your client work. Pick three different conditions or injuries you've treated successfully. For each one, write a simple before-and-after: what the person came in with, what you did, and what changed. Keep it under 200 words per post. Name the condition, name the technique if you're comfortable doing so, and name the outcome.

These posts do two things. First, they show people what you actually do and what results you get. Second, they give you a template you can repeat. Every time you help someone recover from something, you have a new post. You're not inventing content, you're documenting the work you're already doing.

Post them wherever you're already showing up, Instagram, LinkedIn, a newsletter, wherever. Don't wait to build an audience first. The audience comes from seeing the work. If you're building a service-based business, the fastest way to get clients is to show people you've already helped other clients. These three posts are proof.

Once you've posted them, add a simple call to action at the end of each one. Book a session, download a recovery guide, whatever the next step is for someone who wants to work with you. The content is the entry point. The call to action is the bridge to the business you're building.

08
Visual
Identity
A compass for the visual work ahead. Direction for how the visual world should feel.
Mood
Grounded, warm, and quietly confident
Your visual mood should feel calm, capable, and approachable. Think earthy tones, soft pinks, warm neutrals. The kind of space that feels like a treatment room you'd actually want to walk into. Nothing clinical or sterile, nothing overly bright or performative. Just steady, warm, and real. The photography should feel the same way: natural light, simple compositions, close-ups of hands working, tools laid out, spaces that feel inviting without trying too hard.
Photography Style
Simple, close, and focused on the work
Your photography should be straightforward and human. Close-up shots of bodywork tools, hands in motion, treatment spaces that feel calm and uncluttered. Natural light whenever possible. No overly staged lifestyle shots, no stock photo energy. Just real images of the work you do and the spaces you do it in. If you're sharing client recovery stories, a simple photo of the tool or technique you used works better than trying to photograph the person. Keep it focused on the process, the method, the thing that actually changed.
Color Direction
Soft pink, terracotta, sage, cream, charcoal
Warm, grounded palette anchored by dusty pink and terracotta, with sage accent for calm healing energy. Cream background keeps it approachable, charcoal text keeps it readable.
Tone of Voice
Calm, direct, and quietly authoritative
Your tone should sound like someone who knows what they're doing and doesn't need to prove it. Calm, clear, and confident. You're not selling, you're sharing what you've learned and what you've seen work. Write the way you'd explain something to a friend who asked you a real question about their pain. No hype, no jargon they wouldn't understand, no need to soften every statement with a disclaimer. Just say the thing. You're trained, you've done the work, and you know what helps. Let that come through in how you write.
