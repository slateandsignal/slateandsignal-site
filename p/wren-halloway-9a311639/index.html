Blind Spot
You are underselling the specificity of what you know.

Your answers show you have a very specific skill. You can get a nervous adult into cold water without frightening them. You understand the first minute panic and how to talk someone through it. That is a particular threshold moment that most coaches skip past or treat as something to just get over with.

The risk is that you will position yourself as a general cold water swimming coach when what you actually have is expertise in the entry point. The first minute is the thing people ask you for help with. The panic two hundred metres offshore and swimming back anyway is the story that gives you authority with people who are genuinely frightened. That specificity is your advantage.

A weekly letter about the coast is a good idea, but if it is too broad it will not build the audience for the small group sessions. The people who need help with the first minute in cold water are not necessarily the same people who want to read about tidal ecology generally. You can write about both, but the letter needs to stay close enough to the entry threshold that the reader who is nervous about cold water feels seen.

The clearest path forward is to lead with the first minute. That is the thing you said you want to talk about, the thing people ask you for help with, and the thing that separates you from other cold water swimming content. The broader coastal material can support that, but it should not dilute it.

07
First
Move
Write the first minute cold water guide and offer the first session.

You are the product right now, which means your first move is to make the thing you described real and put it in front of people. Write a short guide to the first minute in cold water. Not a course. Publish it somewhere people can find it.

At the same time, offer the first small group session. Not as a beta or a test, as the actual thing. Pick a date, pick a location, decide what the session includes, and invite people. You already know how to do this work. You said you want the sessions to be full without advertising, which means word of mouth, which means the first group has to be good enough that someone tells a friend.

The guide and the session should point to each other. The guide shows you understand the first minute in a way most cold water content does not. The session is where people go if they want help getting through it in person. You do not need a full content strategy or a big audience before you do this. You need one piece of writing that proves you know what you are talking about and one session that proves you can deliver.

Once those two things exist, the weekly letter about the coast has a foundation to build from. People who read the guide or take the session are the ones who will want to hear from you every week. Start there.

08
Visual
Identity
A compass for the visual work ahead. Direction for how the visual world should feel.
Mood
Weathered, capable, and calm.
The visual mood should feel like early morning at the harbour. Worn but well kept. No performance, no extreme sport aesthetic. The kind of images that make cold water look possible for an ordinary person who is nervous. Painted hulls, chipped enamel, weathered rope, dawn light on still water. You want someone to look at your brand and feel like they could do this.
Photography Style
Quiet, real, and close to the water.
Photography should be close and low, shot from the shoreline or the water itself. No dramatic cliffs or big skies. Focus on the threshold: the edge of the dock, the first step in, the surface of the water at eye level. Muted natural light, early morning or late afternoon. Show the coast as a place people actually go. Wetsuits hung to dry, a mug on a boat rail, hands adjusting a strap. The kind of images that make someone feel like they are already there.
Color Direction
Harbour at dusk.
Harbour at dusk: deep working blue, weathered hull red, faded dock-metal teal, warm enamel off-white, near-black text. Calm, capable, grounded.
Tone of Voice
Steady, honest, and close to the experience.
Your tone should sound like someone who has been in the water and knows what it feels like. You do not rush past the fear or pretend it is easy. You do not perform toughness. You talk about cold water the way you would talk to someone standing at the edge of the dock who is genuinely nervous. Short sentences. Plain language. No jargon unless you are teaching it. You name what is hard and then you say what to do. You write like someone who learned to swim late and swam back through panic, which means you understand that courage is the decision to stay.
