The Blind Spot Analysis

What happens when you're too close to your own business?

By Raquel Covey, Founder, Slate & Signal

I built an entire business around helping people see things they couldn't see about themselves.

And then I discovered I needed a me for me.

I had spent quite some time developing Slate & Signal, refining the research process and working out how to communicate what I did. I was so immersed in the details of the business that I had somehow managed to lose sight of the very thing that made it interesting.

It's a little like zooming in on a photograph. You want to see something more clearly, so you keep enlarging the image until eventually you're looking at individual pixels and the picture disappears into a blur.

Sometimes you need to zoom back out.

Which, as it happens, is a fairly good explanation of why I include a Blind Spot Analysis in every Signal Profile and Signal Report.

What you can't see from where you're standing

The interesting thing about a blind spot is that it can obscure something particularly valuable about you.

Consider an interior designer who has developed an extraordinary ability to understand how people want to live in their homes.

She spends time with a client, listens to the way they describe their daily life and begins to understand something about how they would like their home to feel. She can translate that understanding into a physical space that feels remarkably personal to the people living in it.

To her, this is simply how she designs a room.

So when she writes about her business, she describes her design aesthetic and the services she offers. Her portfolio is beautiful, but somewhere in the process of explaining what she does, she's left out the very thing that might make someone particularly interested in hiring her.

She hasn't thought to explain it because she assumes it's part of being a good interior designer.

But I would be fascinated to understand how she does it.

And I suspect a prospective client who has spent years trying to make their house feel like home would be rather interested, too.

What I'm looking for

When I develop the Blind Spot Analysis, I'm examining the individual's answers for things they may have overlooked in describing themselves and their work.

Sometimes I notice a considerable discrepancy between the way someone describes what they do and what their experience suggests they're actually capable of doing.

Other times, I find that they've mentioned something particularly interesting without giving it much significance.

They may have spent years developing an approach to their work that has become so familiar to them that they no longer recognize it as an approach at all.

This is where having someone else examine the information becomes useful.

I can ask why they do something a particular way and investigate whether there's something distinctive about it. I can consider how that approach compares with what is already available in their market and whether it addresses a problem that prospective clients are trying to solve.

What initially appeared to be an ordinary detail about the way someone works could turn out to be central to the way we position their brand.

The trouble with explaining what you do

Have you ever visited someone's website, read through their services and still found yourself wondering what exactly they do?

I have. And on a few unfortunate occasions, it has been my own website.

When you understand your work intimately, it's remarkably easy to leave out information that someone encountering it for the first time needs in order to appreciate it.

You know what happens between the moment a client hires you and the moment you deliver the finished work. You understand why certain decisions are made and what goes into producing the result.

Your prospective client has none of that information.

And if your description assumes they do, they may never understand why your particular approach would be valuable to them.

This is one of the practical applications of the Blind Spot Analysis.

It helps us identify where your brand needs to communicate something that you have previously left unsaid.

For our interior designer, that might mean explaining how she develops an understanding of her clients before she begins designing their homes. A prospective client can then appreciate why the experience of working with her might be particularly well suited to what they're looking for.

The research gives us a reason to emphasize that aspect of her work.

And suddenly, we're making much more informed decisions about what belongs on her website.

What to do with the discovery

Once we've identified a blind spot, I want to understand what it means for the brand.

Does it reveal something about the way you work that should be more prominent in how you describe your services? Could it help explain why a particular type of client would benefit from working with you? Does it suggest an opportunity to develop an offer around something you've been doing for years without recognizing its commercial significance?

These are the sorts of decisions the analysis can inform.

Sometimes the discovery requires a relatively small change in how someone communicates their work.

Sometimes it changes our understanding of what the business could be.

And occasionally, as I discovered with Slate & Signal, it gives you a much clearer understanding of the business you've already built.

I think that's an important distinction. The purpose of this investigation isn't to find something wrong with you that needs fixing. I'm interested in what the evidence reveals about you and whether we're making the best use of that information in developing your brand.

You may already have something quite extraordinary to work with.

We just need to get far enough away from the photograph to see it.

What this means for your Signal Report

Your Blind Spot Analysis is specific to you.

It comes from an examination of the information you've shared and considers what the findings mean for the brand you're building. The recommendations help you understand where your perspective or expertise deserves greater attention and how that understanding can inform your positioning.

It's one of the reasons I place so much importance on the investigation that precedes the creative work.

Before I can develop a brand that accurately represents someone, I need to understand what is particularly significant about them.

And sometimes, the most useful discovery is something they could have told me years ago if only someone had thought to ask.
